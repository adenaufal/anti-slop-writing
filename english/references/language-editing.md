# English Language Editing: Meaning-Preserving Repairs

Use these checks after establishing what the source says and what the reader needs. Each example below is a constructed editorial example, not a factual claim or a quotation. The repair is one possible version; keep the original form when it is grammatical, clear, and fits the intended voice.

## 1. Recover the actor when the action matters

**Condition:** Several sentences hide the person or group making a consequential decision, or a nominal phrase obscures the action.

**Before:** “The request was reviewed, and approval was granted.”

**Meaning-preserving after (if the source identifies the review team as the actor for both):** “The review team assessed the request and approved it.”

**Retain when:** The actor is unknown, irrelevant, intentionally withheld, or the affected object deserves focus. Never invent an actor to make passive prose sound active.

## 2. Attach an introductory modifier to its doer

**Condition:** An opening “-ing,” “having,” or “to” phrase describes an action, but the main clause names a different subject.

**Before:** “After reviewing the draft, the missing citation stood out.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The review revealed a missing citation.”

**Retain when:** The main-clause subject really performs the introductory action: “After reviewing the draft, Maya found the missing citation.” Use a named reviewer only when the source identifies that person. If it does not, “The review revealed a missing citation” avoids assigning an actor.

## 3. Check what an “-ing” appendage claims

**Condition:** A comma plus an “-ing” clause follows a complete sentence. Ask whether it means simultaneous action, a result, a cause, or merely commentary. Grammar alone does not establish which relation is intended.

**Before:** “The editor removed two examples, confusing the reader.”

**Meaning-preserving after (if the removal caused the confusion):** “Removing two examples confused the reader.”

**Retain when:** The attachment clearly modifies the subject and the intended relation is supported: “She left the room, carrying the folder.” If it adds an unsupported cause or evaluation, separate the ideas or remove the comment.

## 4. Resolve pronoun reference and scope

**Condition:** “It,” “they,” “this,” or “which” could refer to more than one noun or claim, or the reader cannot tell which group a statement covers.

**Before:** “The editor sent the writer the revised note after they approved it.”

**Meaning-preserving after (if the writer approved the note):** “After the writer approved the revised note, the editor sent it.”

**Retain when:** Nearby context makes the referent unmistakable. If the source does not show who approved what, do not select an interpretation silently; preserve the ambiguity or request context.

## 5. Preserve quantifiers

**Condition:** An edit changes the size or membership of a group.

**Before:** “Some reviewers requested a shorter introduction.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “Some reviewers asked for a shorter introduction.”

**Not equivalent:** “Reviewers requested a shorter introduction.” This can imply the group as a whole. “Most” is not “some”; “not all” does not mean “none.”

**Retain when:** The quantity is known and the original wording expresses it accurately. Do not add a number or strengthen a generalization for smoother prose.

## 6. Preserve modality and degree of certainty

**Condition:** A rewrite removes or strengthens “may,” “can,” “must,” “likely,” “usually,” or another qualifier.

**Before:** “The change may reduce review time.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The change could shorten review time.”

**Not equivalent:** “The change reduces review time.” That states a result as fact.

**Retain when:** The qualifier marks a real limit, possibility, obligation, or convention. Remove it only when the source supports the stronger claim.

## 7. Preserve negation and its scope

**Condition:** A sentence contains “not,” “never,” “only,” “all,” or a negative quantifier. Check exactly which action or group the negative applies to.

**Before:** “The policy does not require every team to publish weekly.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The policy does not require weekly publication from every team.”

**Not equivalent:** “The policy requires no team to publish weekly.” This changes “not every team” into “no team.”

**Retain when:** The source deliberately distinguishes “not all” from “none,” or limits a negative to one clause. If scope is unclear, do not resolve it by stylistic guesswork.

## 8. Make parallel items match

**Condition:** A list coordinates items with the same grammatical role, but one item has a different form that makes the relation hard to follow.

**Before:** “The role involves reviewing requests, clear documentation, and to answer questions.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The role involves reviewing requests, documenting clearly, and answering questions.”

**Retain when:** Different forms mark genuinely different relationships or the list is intentionally fragmentary. Do not force symmetry if it distorts the content.

## 9. Repair comma splices without changing the relation

**Condition:** A comma alone joins two independent clauses.

**Before:** “The draft was concise, the example was hard to follow.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The draft was concise. The example was hard to follow.”

**Other valid repair:** “The draft was concise; the example was hard to follow.” A conjunction such as “but” is also valid only if the source supports that contrast.

**Retain when:** The comma separates an introductory phrase, items in a list, or a dependent clause. A short pause alone does not make a comma splice acceptable in edited prose.

## 10. Replace empty signposting with the actual relation

**Condition:** A transition announces a point, contrast, or conclusion that the sentence does not actually make, or repeats a relation already clear from the content.

**Before:** “Furthermore, the file is read-only. Moreover, it cannot be edited.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The file is read-only and cannot be edited.”

**Meaning-preserving after:** “The file is read-only; it cannot be edited.”

**Retain when:** A transition genuinely guides the reader through a contrast, consequence, concession, or sequence. Do not rotate among “however,” “therefore,” and “moreover” for variety; choose the relation the evidence supports.

### Evidence and limits

Purdue OWL's resources explain the conventional repairs for dangling modifiers, sentence clarity, parallel construction, and comma splices. These are editing references, not claims about authorship: [Dangling Modifiers](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/dangling_modifiers_and_how_to_correct_them.html), [Sentence Clarity](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/sentence_clarity.html), and [Run-ons, Comma Splices, and Fused Sentences](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/runonsentences.html).

For broad discourse patterns, Yen and Chung's 2025 corpus study examines “however” and “while” in 13,482 words of chatbot argumentation; its genre and sampled prompts constrain generalization ([ROCLING 2025 paper](https://aclanthology.org/2025.rocling-main.38/)). Crosthwaite's 2026 study finds that prompt specificity changes metadiscourse frequencies and rhetorical choices, cautioning against treating such patterns as fixed model traits ([The Modern Language Journal](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.70089)). A 2026 *Nature Human Behaviour* experiment found that rewriting could compress writing-complexity variation in tested English datasets and systems; its tested models and prompts do not establish behavior for current model families ([Sourati et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02550-0)). These findings support checking a rewrite against the source and intended voice, not inferring who wrote it.

The studies above do not establish universal frequencies, a current provider-specific style, or a reliable detector. The constructed examples in this guide demonstrate grammar and editing choices only. Prefer a meaning-preserving repair and retain the original when it already serves the reader.
